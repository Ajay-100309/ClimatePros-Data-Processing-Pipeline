1. The catalog is deduplicating well — and still growing

2. But the new-case rate has flattened, not fallen to zero

This is the report's main finding

A catalog approaching saturation would show the new-case rate decaying toward zero. This one decays and then plateaus around 11%. Roughly one in 9 dispatches still introduces a genuinely new root cause after 13,284 of them — so the catalog should be treated as usefully representative but not complete. It is a working knowledge base, not a finished taxonomy.

3. What that implies at full corpus scale

Case growth follows Heaps' law — cases = K × dispatchesβ, the standard model for how a vocabulary grows as a corpus expands. The fit is unusually tight: β = 0.726, R² = 0.9995 across all 13,284 points. A β below 1 is precisely why deduplication works, and it predicts how the catalog scales.

4. Where dispatches drop out

The note filter is doing most of the heavy lifting. Across 14,981 dispatches, 127,381 individual notes were classified and only 30,933 (24.3%) were judged technically useful — about 2.1 useful notes per dispatch out of 8.5 written. Three of every four notes are scheduling, dispatch, or billing chatter.

8. Data-quality findings

The largest "case" is not a fault

CASE-0012 — "No technical fault identified in dispatch notes" — is the biggest case in the catalog with 547 member dispatches. It is the pipeline's own placeholder for a successful run that found no fault, not a diagnosis. Together with similar entries, the No Technical Fault category accounts for 1,656 dispatches (12.5% of everything mapped). Any downstream consumer should filter it out before treating the catalog as a fault taxonomy.

Category labels are fragmenting

The extraction prompt asks for a standardised 2–4 word category, but 266 distinct categories have emerged, and 175 of them (66%) carry 10 or fewer dispatches. Door-related failures alone are split across 14 separate categories (618 dispatches) — Door Seal, Door Gasket, Door Handle, Door Hardware, Door Mechanism and more. Fan and motor failures similarly split across overlapping labels.

Note this affects the category field only — it is metadata, not the matching key. Case identity comes from the root-cause text and the AI judge, so fragmented categories do not merge or split cases. It does mean category counts should be read as indicative, and a normalisation pass would make them reportable.

Category and root cause can disagree

Spot-checking the largest cases surfaces mislabels: CASE-0308 is filed under Condenser Fan Motor Failure, but its canonical text describes a broken blower motor belt. The root-cause text — the field that drives matching — is correct; the category assigned alongside it is not. Same conclusion as above: trust the text, treat the category as a hint.

10. What this supports, and what it doesn't

Supported today. The catalog is a sound basis for recognising repeat work: 86% of dispatches match something already known, the top 100 cases cover roughly half of all resolved dispatches, and the deduplication ratio of 7.3× is stable and improving. Retrieval-style uses — "has this failure been seen before, and what was done about it" — are well served.

Not yet supported. Treating the catalog as complete. At 1.03% of the corpus with a new-case rate still near 11%, roughly one dispatch in 9 is still novel, and the Heaps fit puts the eventual catalog in the tens of thousands of cases. Frequency statistics drawn from it describe the sample, not the business — and the category field needs normalisation before it can carry reporting.
